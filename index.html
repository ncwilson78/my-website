Nicholas Wilson, Ed.D.
Learning scientist and builder. Director of Learning Innovation at Harvard University.

What counts as evidence that someone is learning, and how should a system, or a designer, respond to it?

I have worked on that question for more than 15 years across K-12 classrooms, university teaching, and workforce training: as a physics teacher, as a learning sciences researcher, as a co-principal investigator on NSF-funded augmented reality tutors, as the director of university-wide teaching innovation at Harvard, and now as Director of Learning Innovation at Harvard University. This site collects that work, the evidence behind it, and the places where the evidence changed my mind.

Statements on this site carry a marker when they are not settled by a cited source or a direct measurement, so the strength of each claim is visible.

Work

Three case studies, each organized around the question it started with, what I built, what the evidence showed, and what changed my thinking.

  1. 2026 to present
    Harvard University

    Evidence of learning in courses without grades

    Which observable behaviors justify a claim that someone understood something, and what should a system say when none do?

    An evidence-centered model for contingent reasoning in courses that carry no grades, with an explicit inferential distance on every link between a signal and a claim, built only from teaching elements the platform already has.

  2. 2026 to present
    Harvard University

    Staff as learners: an AI course-design system

    If an AI authoring tool is part of how colleagues learn to build courses, what should it leave to them?

    A multi-agent course-design system I designed and built, studied through design-based research with the instructional designers and technologists who use it, whose practice redirected the product three times.

  3. 2019 to 2023
    Northeastern University

    When learners outgrew the tool

    What should a tutor do once the learner no longer needs it?

    NSF-funded augmented reality tutors for manufacturing work, where a guide that cut assembly errors by 31 to 84 percent turned out to be working as training, which moved the research toward scaffolding that recognizes expertise and fades.

How I work

These are the habits I try to hold, each with the experience that taught it to me.

Studying the activity before designing for it
Before any staff AI training existed in my office, the AI Inventory documented how teams actually used AI, and the gaps people described concerned rules, guidance, and whom to ask more than the tools themselves. The course that followed was built around those gaps.
Letting what learners do revise the design
Participants in the first AR study outgrew the guide after two or three attempts, and Harvard course-design staff kept arriving with courses they had already written. Both observations reorganized the systems I was building around them.
Deciding where humans matter most before deciding what to automate
An instructional technologist explained that building pages by hand is where they ask whether each element is the right one. The response was to automate the checking, keep the building in human hands by default, and make build automation opt-in.
Giving the model judgment and giving code the guarantees
The model decides what to teach, in what order, and how to phrase it. Citations, structure, and platform settings are checked deterministically, a division I adopted after a prompt-level fix failed for a defect my own code was causing.
Using research as a constraint where it is strong and as a hypothesis where AI changes its conditions
Worked examples, retrieval practice, and feedback shape the staff course directly. Assessment practices built for learners without a capable assistant are treated as hypotheses to retest.
Holding favorite ideas provisionally
I removed a capstone thread I liked from the staff course because it broke the 15-minute cap, and I rebuilt the Course Building Assistant around manuscript import, which demoted the generation path I had built first.
Asking whether a technology narrows gaps between learners or widens them
That question started with my dissertation on technology integration and student use in a one-to-one laptop school, and it still shapes how I evaluate learning technology.

Ideas I want to test

  • Grading the change in a learner's reasoning. Learners commit to a position, check it against an AI, and explain where and why they changed their mind, and the quality of that explanation becomes the evidence. open question
  • A tutor that asks for a commitment first. It would ask for a prediction or rationale before revealing anything, track how confidence and reasoning shift across varied situations, and fade its own help as those signals strengthen. expectation
  • A competency graph that carries its own uncertainty. Learner state would be represented per competency together with the inferential distance behind each estimate, and agent behavior would read from that state. open question
  • Crediting the decision not to use AI. A staff AI competency framework from this work credits a well-reasoned decision not to use AI as highly as adoption, which treats judgment as the competency.
  • A conversation among faculty who were never recorded together. A multivocal framework, now in progress, flags where each discipline's perspective belongs so that a course can carry several faculty voices through one coherent argument.
