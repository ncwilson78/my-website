AI-Assisted Course Authoring as Workplace Learning

Most AI authoring tools are judged by how much time they save. When course teams are learning to design with AI, however, the tool also shapes which parts of the craft they continue to practice and which they hand off, and so its design is, arguably, as much a question of workplace learning as of productivity.

Context

The Course Building Assistant is a multi-agent course-design system used by course teams developing online learning solutions for various audiences, including a growing set of Harvard Online course builds. Its users are instructional designers, instructional technologists, and faculty, and much of what they are learning right now is how to build courses with AI. In practice, then, the system is part of their curriculum, and I study its use the way I would study any learning setting. The guiding question has been twofold: which parts of course design should the system perform, and which should remain with the people whose expertise it is meant to extend?

The system

I designed and built the system with Claude Code over roughly six months, moving from concept to institutional use across eight releases between June and September 2026 (with 437 automated tests along the way). Specialized agents work through three human approval gates, drawing on eight pedagogical styles, a taxonomy of 20 teaching-element types, and course outlines whose units are linked to prerequisites and outcomes. Four evaluators (a blind, multi-persona review, pedagogical fidelity, narrative coherence, and copyediting) review each draft, each informed by deterministic pre-scans. A manuscript-to-build-document pipeline is now in pilot with instructional technologists, and a build guide, in-app manual, and MIT-licensed, self-hosted distribution allow other teams to run and adapt the system on their own.

Studying use as learning

The work follows a design-based research approach, in which each release functions as a conjecture to be tested in practice and revised in light of what practitioners actually do.1 On the qualitative side, I rely on interviews and conversations with course team members, analytic memos, and field notes from build sessions, using a grounded theory approach that allows categories to emerge from the data.2 Cultural-Historical Activity Theory then frames how those categories relate, by situating the tensions among them within an activity system of people, tools, rules, and divisions of labor.3 On the quantitative side, usage logs record how each generated element was kept, revised, or regenerated, alongside time to build.

This lens comes directly from my earlier research on technology-rich classrooms, which found that new tools and tasks alone did little to change participation, and that what mattered was the coupling of changes to tools, rules, and divisions of labor, particularly when that coupling legitimized the knowledge participants already brought with them.4,5 Course teams, it turns out, bring a great deal.

What changed the design

Augmenting faculty expertise

Reviews of early modules surfaced a recurring problem: when given a faculty member's existing prose, the system tended to rewrite it rather than build on it. A production colleague questioned the very premise of generating course content from scratch, and the team converged on the principle that AI should augment faculty work rather than substitute for it. I codified that principle in the governing build guide as an augmentation mode, and we now track four recurring defects by count rather than by impression (i.e., replacing faculty voice, fragmenting interview transcripts, fabricating citations or frameworks, and introducing framework labels where the design calls for induction). A current pilot compares two model configurations for one agent role, scored blind against those counts, with the decision rule specified before any results are in.

Designing around authorship

Fieldwork also showed that many course teams begin with a manuscript they have already written, and that early versions of the system could overwrite their revisions when they uploaded an edited document. Viewed through an activity lens, those manuscripts are the teams' funds of knowledge, and the system was effectively de-legitimizing them. Manuscript import is now the primary workflow: five deterministic parsers bring a written course into the platform's structure without an AI call, edits survive a round trip exactly, and human-authored manuscripts are exempt from automated AI review by default. Over several months, the system shifted from an author that staff supervised to an assistant organized around their authorship, and each of those changes was a product decision and a learning design decision at once.

Locating human judgment

Perhaps the most consequential finding came from instructional technologists, who described building pages by hand as the moment when they ask whether each element is the right teaching choice in the first place. Seen this way, the build is a site of reflection-in-action as much as production,6 and automating it wholesale would save hours while, I expect, removing an important source of course quality. Our response was to automate verification rather than construction (producing a punch list of remaining issues for a finished build) and to make build automation optional. A generated build document reduces the technologist's step to an estimated 30-minute review, and because adoption is optional, efficiency gains are best described as an adoption curve rather than a single figure. The optional design also creates a natural comparison between teams who automate and teams who build by hand.

Carrying several voices

The same attention to how designers work informs a multivocal framework now in development, built for courses whose faculty, drawn from different disciplines, were never recorded together. The framework flags where each discipline's perspective belongs in the narrative, so that a course can carry several faculty voices through a single, coherent argument.

A related principle, separating what the model should judge from what code should guarantee, is the subject of Humans as Architects in AI-Assisted Course Design.
