Measuring Learning in Ungraded Courses: An Evidence-Centered Approach

Completion records indicate that a learner was exposed to a course, not what the learner came to understand. This work develops an evidence model for ungraded courses that specifies what can, and cannot, be claimed from the signals such courses produce.

Context

Much professional and non-degree learning carries no grades, and the default record of a learner is a sequence of pages viewed, videos watched, and activities submitted. Organizations that invest in such learning increasingly ask what their people actually learned, a question completion data cannot answer. One of the workstreams I direct addresses this problem, beginning with non-degree courses offered through my office and including a proposed pilot to provide organizational clients with defensible evidence of learning.

Design

The model follows evidence-centered design, which distinguishes the claims to be made about a learner, the observations that would support those claims, and the tasks that would elicit those observations.1 The target construct is contingent reasoning: understanding when an approach applies, when it does not, and why. This construct is closer to what organizations value than recall of a framework, and it draws on research showing that explaining one's reasoning is itself a powerful learning process.2

  • Observations. Before and after instruction, learners commit to a position on a scenario, rate their confidence, and provide a brief rationale scored against a rubric, which supports claims about both reasoning and calibration.
  • Tasks. All tasks are constructed from teaching elements the platform already supports, so the model can operate within existing courses without new platform development.
  • Inferential distance. Every link between a signal and a claim about understanding carries an explicit statement of how far the inference extends, so that weak evidence cannot be reported as strong evidence.

The table below presents a simplified illustration of how the model treats different signals.

Design commitments

Instrumentation before measurement. A colleague and I are mapping, in Apache Superset, what each teaching element on the platform actually records before any measure is defined, because a measure that depends on data the platform never captures would fail silently while still producing a number.

Absence of evidence reported as such. A learner who omits the rationale has provided no evidence of reasoning, and the model is designed to report that absence rather than infer a level from completion or time on page.

Defined limits for synthetic evaluation. Simulated learners and LLM judges can help screen whether a task is clear and appropriately paced, but they cannot provide evidence that people learned, so claims about learning are reserved for real learners, with human review of rubric scoring.

Status and open questions

Telemetry mapping is underway, and the feasibility pilot proposal is under review. Several questions remain open: whether learners in ungraded courses will commit and explain at rates sufficient to support claims about a cohort, how closely model-assisted rubric scoring agrees with trained human raters on brief rationales, and how the inferential distances in the model should be validated empirically rather than set by design judgment alone.

The same evidence model underlies the competency graph and tutoring design described in Evidence of Learning When AI Is Ubiquitous.
