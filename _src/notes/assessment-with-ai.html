Note, October 2026

Assessment when every learner has an assistant

When an AI can produce a competent answer on request, a correct answer says little about what the learner can do. These notes describe how I have handled that problem in a staff course I wrote, and the ideas I would most like to test next.

Judgment as the evidence

Working with AI at VPAL is a staff course of 15-minute, text-only sections built around one routine drawn from VPAL's acceptable use policy: Decide, Ask, Check, Own. Its learners have AI available for every task, so the course does not treat the output as evidence of anything. It treats the learner's judgment about the output as the evidence.

In one activity, learners mark six AI outputs as "looks right" or "not sure" before seeing the sources. One of the six contains no error, so the exercise cannot be passed by assuming everything is wrong, and the pattern of marks shows whether a learner's suspicion is calibrated or indiscriminate. The staff competency framework behind the course follows the same logic and credits a well-reasoned decision not to use AI as highly as adoption.

Testing the first draft against five leading introductory AI courses exposed my own assumptions. Its examples came mostly from administrative work, although the AI Inventory had mapped seven functional areas, and its verification practice gave learners too little feedback to learn from a missed error. The second version addressed both, and I removed a capstone thread I liked because it broke the 15-minute cap and made later sections depend on entries learners might not see again.

The course is with the AI Leadership Team for review and has not met learners yet. Whether its calibration activity changes how often staff check AI output in their real work is the outcome that matters, and it is unmeasured. open question

Grading the change in a learner's reasoning

The idea I would most like to test extends that logic to assessment generally. A learner commits to a position, checks it against an AI, and then explains where and why they changed their mind, or why they did not. The answer itself carries little weight, and the evidence is the quality of the reasoning about the change: whether the learner can say what the AI's response revealed, what conditions would make their original position right after all, and what they would do differently in a new case.

This design treats the AI as part of the task rather than a threat to it, and it connects directly to the contingent-reasoning measures in the evidence model for ungraded courses. My expectation is that explanations anchored to a learner's own earlier commitment are harder to outsource than answers, expectation although a capable assistant could also produce a plausible account of a change of mind, and finding out how easily it can is the first experiment I would run. open question

A tutor that asks first

In an AI-native product, the same idea would shape agent behavior. The tutor would ask the learner to commit to a prediction or a rationale before it reveals anything, track how confidence and reasoning change across varied situations, and fade its own help as those signals strengthen. The fading principle comes from my AR tutor research, where participants outgrew a guide that never adjusted to them.

In system terms, I would extend the prerequisite and outcome links that the Course Building Assistant's outlines already carry into an explicit competency graph. The evidence model would attach to its nodes, so that learner state is represented per competency together with the inferential distance behind each estimate, and the tutor's behavior would read from that state. A node with only distant evidence would prompt the tutor to elicit better evidence before advancing, rather than treating completion as mastery. expectation

Where research constrains the design and where it becomes a hypothesis

I let research constrain the work where it is strong. Worked examples, retrieval practice, and feedback that arrives while a learner can still act on it shape the staff course directly, and evidence-centered design governs how I connect observations to claims.1 I treat research as a hypothesis where AI changes the conditions it was built on, and assessment is the clearest case, because most of the practices we inherited assume a learner working without a capable assistant at hand.

What would change my mind

  • If explanations of a changed position turn out to be as easy to generate with AI as answers, the evidence collapses, and the design would need to move toward process evidence gathered during the work or toward conversation with a person.
  • If calibration in the course does not carry over to how staff check AI output in their real work, judgment exercises of this kind measure performance in the exercise and little else.
  • If LLM-assisted scoring of rationales diverges from trained human raters, the scoring has to stay human until the gap is understood.
