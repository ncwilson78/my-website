Model Judgment and Deterministic Verification in AI-Assisted Course Authoring

Developing the Course Building Assistant, an AI course-design system used by course teams developing online learning solutions for various audiences, led me to a working principle for AI authoring systems: language models should carry responsibility for pedagogical judgment, while any requirement that can be verified should be guaranteed by deterministic code.

An instructive defect

Early in development, generated modules repeatedly included transition text that previewed upcoming videos, contrary to explicit instructions in the prompt. Successive refinements to those instructions, including a self-check the model performed before producing output, did not resolve the problem. Tracing the output showed that the text originated in a deterministic post-processing step that assembled transitions between consecutive videos, rather than in the model's output. The remedy was a change to that step, together with a sanitization pass applied to every transition regardless of its source.

The episode was minor in itself, but it exposed a broader design tendency worth correcting: relying on instructions to a probabilistic system for guarantees that properly belong in code.

A division of labor

I now classify each requirement according to whether it calls for judgment or admits verification. The model decides what to teach, in what sequence, and how to express it, while code ensures what can be checked.

  • Citations. Testing showed that models could carry stale video timecodes over from elsewhere in source documents. A verifier now matches each video reference against its transcript and corrects the range, leaving ambiguous cases unchanged and flagged for human review.
  • Structure. Faculty and staff manuscripts are imported through five deterministic parsers without an AI call, and any content a parser cannot classify is preserved and flagged rather than discarded.
  • Platform constraints. Analysis of the platform's actual export format identified settings that the system had been asking the model to supply but that the platform does not support, and these were removed from every prompt and example.
  • Evaluation. Deterministic scans for repeated activities and inconsistent naming are passed to the LLM evaluators as evidence to interpret, so the evaluators reason about verified counts rather than attempting to reproduce them.

The same principle governs where AI runs at all. Human-authored manuscripts are exempt from automated AI review by default, and automated insertions that suit generated modules are disabled for content meant to be imported verbatim.

Implications for the people in the loop

Each requirement moved into code is also a decision about what reviewers no longer need to check and where their attention should go. When citations and structure are verified deterministically, instructional designers can concentrate on whether a sequence teaches well, the judgment that most depends on their expertise. My expectation is that this reallocation of attention matters as much for course quality as any gain in speed, although it has not yet been measured.

Open questions

The automated evaluators produce scores anchored to quoted evidence, but their agreement with experienced instructional designers rating the same modules has not yet been measured, and published work on LLM-as-judge evaluation documents position, verbosity, and self-preference biases that warrant caution.1 I expect agreement to vary by dimension, higher for structural properties such as workload and alignment and lower for whether a sequence will sustain a particular learner's engagement. Until that relationship is established, evaluator scores serve as a screen before human review and are not treated as evidence of learning.
