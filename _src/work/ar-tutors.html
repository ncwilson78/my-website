Case study, Northeastern University, 2019 to 2023

From Assistance to Adaptive Scaffolding: Augmented Reality Tutors for Psychomotor Skill

An NSF-funded program of research on augmented reality (AR) guidance for manufacturing work, which moved from evaluating AR as performance support to modeling expertise as the basis for scaffolding that recedes as competence develops.

Context

Manufacturing increasingly asks workers to perform complex assembly and inspection tasks with little formal training, and AR offers a way to place instruction directly on the work. The project began with an applied question about whether AR guidance improves accuracy relative to paper instructions. It also carried a learning sciences question that became central over time: whether a system designed to assist performance should be designed differently from one intended to build skill, and how a system could tell which of the two a particular person needs.

Approach

As a co-principal investigator on an NSF Future of Work grant, I worked within an interdisciplinary team of engineers, computer scientists, and learning scientists. The first study compared a static AR guide with paper instructions in an assembly task.1 Subsequent studies instrumented AR-guided tasks with eye tracking, hand tracking, and galvanic skin response, comparing experts and novices to characterize expertise as it unfolds during the work rather than only in its outcomes.2,3,4

Findings

The AR guide reduced assembly errors by 31 to 84 percent relative to paper instructions.1 Participants also reported relying on the guide less after two or three attempts, which indicated that a system designed as ongoing assistance was functioning as training. In the expertise study, experts scanned the workspace more quickly, required less instructional support, and worked with greater accuracy and efficiency, while novices showed a positive correlation between perceived performance and skin-response amplitude that experts did not.3 A subsequent journal article developed these signals into a multimodal assessment of expertise.4

Interpretation

The findings are consistent with a long line of work on scaffolding, in which support is calibrated to the learner and withdrawn as competence develops,5,6 and with the expertise reversal effect, in which guidance that helps novices can impede more experienced performers.7 A static guide cannot fade, so the design problem shifted from presenting instructions well to recognizing expertise as it develops.

Continuity with current work

The principle that support should respond to evidence about the learner shapes my current work. The evidence model for courses without assignments or instructor-student interaction states the inferential distance behind every claim about a learner, and the tutoring design described in Evidence of Learning When AI Is Ubiquitous adopts fading as a core behavior.
