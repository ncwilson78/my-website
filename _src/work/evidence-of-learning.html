Case study, Harvard University, 2026 to present

Evidence of learning in courses without grades

Completion data shows that someone was exposed to a course. Organizations that send their people through courses and training programs increasingly want to know what those people learned, and in an ungraded course the evidence for that claim has to be designed.

The question at the start

Much of the learning adults do for work carries no grades, and the default record of a learner is a trail of pages viewed, videos watched, and activities submitted. assumption One of the three workstreams I direct at Harvard asks how to measure learning rather than completion in courses like these, starting with the University's non-degree courses, including a pilot proposal to give enterprise clients defensible evidence of what their people learned. The question underneath is which observable behaviors justify a claim that someone understood something, and what a system should say when none do.

What I designed

The model follows evidence-centered design, which separates what we want to claim about a learner, what observations would support the claim, and which tasks would elicit those observations.1 The construct is contingent reasoning: knowing when an approach applies and when it does not, which is closer to what organizations care about than recall of a framework.

  • Observations. Before and after instruction, learners commit to a position on a scenario, rate their confidence, and give a short rationale scored against a rubric.
  • Tasks. Every task is built from teaching elements the platform already has, so the model can run in existing courses without new platform features.
  • Inferential distance. Every link between a signal and a claim about understanding carries an explicit statement of how far the inference reaches, so that a weak signal cannot be reported as strong evidence.

Decisions that shaped the model

The order of work mattered more than I first assumed. A colleague and I are mapping, in Apache Superset, what each teaching element on the platform actually records before defining any measure, because a measure that depends on a field the platform never writes would fail silently and still produce a number.

Missing evidence is treated as information, since a learner who skips the rationale has produced no evidence of reasoning, and the model is designed to report that absence rather than infer a level from completion or time on page, so being explicit when no signal justifies a claim is part of the design.

Synthetic evaluation has a defined and limited place in the model. Simulated learners and LLM judges are useful for screening whether a task is clear and well paced, but they cannot produce evidence that real people learned, so the model reserves claims about learning for real learners, with human review of the rubric scoring.

What I do not know yet

  • Whether learners in an ungraded course will commit and explain at rates high enough to support claims about a cohort. open question
  • How well model-assisted rubric scoring agrees with trained human raters on short rationales. open question
  • The feasibility pilot proposal is under review and has not yet run.
