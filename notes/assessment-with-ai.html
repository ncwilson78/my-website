Essay, October 2026

Evidence of Learning When AI Is Ubiquitous

When a capable AI system can produce a correct answer on request, the answer alone carries limited information about what the learner knows or can do. This essay outlines an approach that treats judgment, calibration, and the revision of reasoning as primary evidence, and describes how that approach informs current design work and a program of future research.

The assessment problem

Most assessment practice rests on an assumption that the learner produced the work being evaluated. Ubiquitous AI weakens that assumption for a wide range of tasks, and the familiar responses, restricting AI or detecting its use, protect the old inference without asking what evidence would now be meaningful. Evidence-centered design offers a more productive starting point, because it begins with the claim to be made about a learner and works backward to the observations that would support it.1 Applied to learners who have AI at hand, it shifts attention from the products learners submit to the judgments they make about AI-assisted work and the ways their reasoning changes.

Judgment as evidence

A staff AI course I designed for my office applies this reasoning to workplace learning. The course consists of 15-minute, text-only sections organized around a single routine drawn from the office's acceptable use policy: decide, ask, check, and own. Because learners have AI available for every task, the course treats their judgment about AI output as the evidence of learning. In one activity, learners classify six AI outputs as plausible or uncertain before consulting the sources, and one of the six contains no error, so the activity rewards calibrated scrutiny rather than blanket suspicion. The staff competency framework behind the course follows the same logic and credits a well-reasoned decision not to use AI as highly as effective adoption.

Before the course reached learners, I reviewed the draft against five widely used introductory AI courses. The review showed that its examples drew too heavily on administrative work, despite an organizational inventory that had mapped seven functional areas, and that its verification exercises gave learners too little feedback to learn from an error they missed. The second version addressed both issues, and I also removed a capstone component because it exceeded the section length and created dependencies across sections that learners might not revisit. The course is currently with the office's AI Leadership Team for review, and whether its calibration activities change how carefully staff check AI output in their daily work remains to be studied.

Assessing the revision of reasoning

The research direction I find most promising extends this logic to assessment generally. A learner commits to a position, consults an AI, and then explains whether and why the position changed. The evidence lies less in the final answer than in the quality of that explanation: whether the learner can identify what the AI's response revealed, under what conditions the original position would still hold, and how the reasoning would apply to a new case. The design draws on established work on self-explanation,2 on conceptual change as the accommodation of new ideas against prior ones,3 and on predict-observe-explain tasks as probes of understanding.4

The approach incorporates AI into the task rather than treating it as a threat to the task, and it connects directly to the contingent-reasoning measures in the evidence model for ungraded courses. My working hypothesis is that explanations anchored to a learner's own prior commitment are more difficult to outsource than answers. A capable assistant could nonetheless generate a plausible account of a change of mind, and determining how readily it can is the first study this approach requires.

Implications for AI tutoring systems

In an AI-native learning environment, the same principle would govern agent behavior. A tutor would ask the learner to commit to a prediction or rationale before revealing anything, track how confidence and reasoning change across varied situations, and reduce its support as those signals strengthen. The emphasis on fading follows from research on scaffolding and from my earlier work on augmented reality tutors, in which participants outgrew a guide that could not adapt to them.5

At the level of system architecture, this implies extending the prerequisite and outcome links that the Course Building Assistant's outlines already carry into an explicit competency graph. The evidence model would attach to each node, so that learner state is represented per competency together with the inferential distance behind each estimate, and the tutor's behavior would draw on that state. Where a competency is supported only by distant evidence, the tutor would seek better evidence before advancing the learner, rather than treating completion as mastery.

Research as constraint and as hypothesis

I treat research as a constraint where its findings are robust. Worked examples,6 retrieval practice,7 and timely, task-focused feedback8 shape the staff course directly. I treat research as a hypothesis where AI alters the conditions under which it was established, and assessment is the clearest case, because most inherited assessment practices assume a learner working without a capable assistant.

Conditions that would revise this approach

  • If explanations of a changed position prove as easy to generate with AI as answers, this form of evidence loses its value, and assessment would need to rely more on process evidence gathered during the work or on dialogue with a person.
  • If calibration within the course does not transfer to how staff evaluate AI output in their daily work, judgment exercises of this kind measure performance within the exercise and little beyond it.
  • If LLM-assisted scoring of rationales diverges from trained human raters, scoring should remain with people until the source of the divergence is understood.
