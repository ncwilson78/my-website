Essay, October 2026

Evidence of Learning When AI Is Ubiquitous

It should come as no surprise that much of the conversation about AI and assessment has centered on detection and restriction. I think the more productive question is a design question: when a learner can produce a competent answer on request, what kinds of activity would still give us credible evidence of what that learner knows and can do?

Assessment as an activity system

My own research has relied heavily on Cultural-Historical Activity Theory, which treats learning as activity mediated by tools, rules, communities, and divisions of labor.1 Viewed through that lens, generative AI is not simply a new tool that learners bring to an old task. Rather, it changes the relationships among every element of the activity, and the tensions that follow (e.g., between the rule that work be one's own and a tool that can do much of the work) are less a matter of student misconduct than a signal that the activity itself needs redesign. In earlier work on technology-rich classrooms, I argued that isolated changes to tools or tasks are rarely sufficient, and that it is the coupling of changes to tasks, rules, and mediating artifacts that opens new and more legitimate forms of participation.2 Assessment, arguably, now faces the same challenge.

Evidence-centered design offers a practical way to work through it, since it begins with the claim we want to make about a learner and works backward to the observations and tasks that would support that claim.3 Applied to learners who have AI at hand, the approach shifts attention away from the products learners submit and toward the judgments they make about AI-assisted work, as well as the ways their reasoning changes in the process.

Judgment as evidence

A staff AI course I designed for my office takes this approach in a workplace learning setting. The course consists of 15-minute, text-only sections organized around a single routine drawn from the office's acceptable use policy (decide, ask, check, and own). Because learners have AI available for every task, the course treats their judgment about AI output as the evidence of learning. In one activity, for example, learners classify six AI outputs as "looks right" or "not sure" before seeing the sources. One of the six contains no error at all, so the exercise cannot be passed by assuming everything is wrong, and the pattern of responses shows whether a learner's skepticism is calibrated or simply indiscriminate. The competency framework behind the course follows the same logic and credits a well-reasoned decision not to use AI as highly as effective adoption.

Before the course reached learners, I reviewed the draft against five widely used introductory AI courses, and the comparison exposed a few of my own assumptions. Its examples leaned heavily on administrative work (despite an organizational inventory that had mapped seven functional areas), and its verification exercises gave learners too little feedback to learn from an error they had missed. The second version addressed both issues, and I removed a capstone component I was fond of because it exceeded the section length and created dependencies across sections that learners might not revisit.

Grading the change

The direction I find most promising extends this logic to assessment more broadly. A learner commits to a position, consults an AI, and then explains whether, and why, the position changed. The evidence lies less in the final answer than in the quality of that explanation: whether the learner can identify what the AI's response revealed, under what conditions the original position might still hold, and how the reasoning would apply to a new case. The design draws on well-established work on self-explanation,4 on conceptual change as the accommodation of new ideas against prior ones,5 and on predict-observe-explain tasks as probes of understanding.6 It also brings AI into the activity as a mediating artifact, rather than treating it as a threat to be managed. My working hypothesis is that explanations anchored to a learner's own prior commitment are considerably harder to outsource than answers, and testing that hypothesis is the first study I would want to run.

What this means for AI tutors

In an AI-native learning environment, the same principle would shape how agents behave. A tutor would ask the learner to commit to a prediction or rationale before revealing anything, track how confidence and reasoning change across varied situations, and reduce its support as those signals strengthen. The emphasis on fading comes from research on scaffolding7 and from my earlier work on augmented reality tutors, in which participants outgrew a guide that could not adapt to them.

Architecturally, this would mean extending the prerequisite and outcome links that our course outlines already carry into an explicit competency graph. The evidence model would attach to each node, so that learner state is represented per competency together with the inferential distance behind each estimate, and the tutor's behavior would draw on that state. Where a competency is supported only by distant evidence (e.g., completion or time on page), the tutor would seek better evidence before advancing the learner, rather than treating completion as mastery.

When research constrains, and when it becomes a hypothesis

I let research constrain the design where its findings are robust; worked examples,8 retrieval practice,9 and timely, task-focused feedback10 all shape the staff course directly. Where AI changes the conditions under which a body of research was established, however, I treat that research as a hypothesis to retest, and assessment is perhaps the clearest case, since most of the practices we have inherited assume a learner working without a capable assistant at hand.

As we design learning experiences for this moment, questions worth asking might include: What claim about the learner does this activity actually support once AI is present? Which elements of the activity (i.e., its rules, tools, and division of labor between learner and AI) need to change together for that claim to hold? And how might the activity treat a learner's judgment about AI, including the decision not to use it, as evidence in its own right?
