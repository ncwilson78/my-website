Learning in Pieces: What a Brain Injury Reveals About Knowledge and AI

A motorcycle accident gave a music teacher perfect pitch, and it faded as his brain healed. His case shows how much of learning is filtering and connecting what we know. AI tools can now do some of that work for us, which raises a question for anyone building them: how do we help learners organize their own knowledge, starting from the pieces they bring?

An ear that changed overnight

Before a motorcycle accident left him with a brain injury, Edward Freytag had been a working musician for forty years and taught middle school music. Like most trained musicians, he heard music through the intervals between notes. About two weeks after the accident, a jazz recording came on the car radio and he started naming its chords as they went by, complex ones included, something he had never been able to do. He saw them written out as sheet music in his head. His brain identified the pitch the way ours identifies the color red.

That ability, absolute pitch, shows up in roughly one in 10,000 people. It appears to be largely inherited, adults almost never learn it, and the few who have it, Mozart and Ella Fitzgerald among them, are often prodigies. For many of them, notes first show up as colors, a form of synesthesia that points to a brain wired differently. Freytag acquired it as an adult, almost overnight. Sadie Dingfelder's article in The New York Times Magazine, "The Brain Injury That Made Edward Freytag a Musical Genius," describes his as the first documented case of this happening after a brain injury.1

As his brain healed, the ability faded. So did a burst of creativity that had come with it: after the accident he had been getting up in the middle of the night to compose music he didn't remember writing. His hearing returned to the ordinary way, tracking how notes relate to each other and letting the exact pitch slip into the background.

That kind of filtering is familiar. We remember the gist of a conversation and very few of the exact words, and a mind that held onto every detail would struggle to find the ones that matter. Learning works in a similar way. We connect new knowledge to what we already know, figure out which details are important, and set the rest aside.

Learning an instrument makes this easy to see. A beginning guitarist focuses on specific details: where to place their fingers on the fretboard to make a chord, which notes to play in a melody, how to play a song note for note like the original. With practice, they notice that many songs share the same handful of chords, often in the same order, and strings of notes start to look like parts of a scale in a particular key. Most players stop around there.

The few who keep going can do something remarkable. A jazz guitarist can improvise as a song's chords and key change. They can hear what the pianist just played and answer it, the way you'd respond to someone in conversation. The details of fingers on the fretboard fade, giving way to the song's structure and to what the player wants to express. Researchers who study expertise have found that the difference between experts and beginners is as much about how their knowledge is organized as about how many details they know.2, 3 Expert knowledge across domains—not just music—is connected around a few deep patterns, which helps experts notice what matters and use what they know in situations they've never seen before.

Building that kind of knowledge takes years of this filtering. What feels new is that AI tools can pull up almost any detail in seconds, and they can do some of the filtering for us. People are now building learning tools with them. So one question for those of us staring into the future of education in the age of AI is whether those tools help learners develop how their knowledge is organized, by helping them do their own filtering, or whether the tools do the filtering for them, so they never have to. If it's the second, learners might sound fluent while what they know stays in pieces.

Knowledge in pieces

Most guitar players go through a stage where they can play a song comfortably in one spot on the neck and stumble when they try it anywhere else. The song hasn't changed, and the notes relate to each other in exactly the same way, but what they've learned is still tied to particular finger positions. Over time, for many players, those positions start to connect, and the song becomes something they can find anywhere on the instrument. Even experienced players carry some knowledge like this, solid in one setting and shaky in another.

The education researcher Andrea diSessa found something similar in how people understand the physical world. He spent years interviewing students about everyday motion, like a ball tossed in the air or a book sliding across a table.4 A student might explain one situation well, then reason quite differently about another that works the same way. For decades, educators called answers like these misconceptions, wrong ideas to be found and replaced.5 DiSessa and his colleagues saw something more useful. Our sense of how the world works is built from many small intuitions, like the feeling that things need a push to keep moving. Each one works well in some situations and gets applied in others where it doesn't fit. He called this "knowledge in pieces."6 David Hammer pushed the idea further, describing students' intuitions as resources that get switched on by context, which makes a so-called mistake look more like a good idea turning up in the wrong place.7

Seeing intuitions this way changes the job of teaching. If a student's intuitions are pieces that work in some situations, they become the material to build from. Learning means sorting out which intuition applies where, and connecting the pieces so they hold up in situations the student hasn't seen yet. As those connections form, something new takes shape. A student meets a new idea, like friction, and it changes how the old intuitions work, sharpening some and limiting others to the situations where they belong. A student who once saw a table as simply in the way of a resting book starts to see the table pushing back. Part of what the student learns, too, is what to stop paying attention to. Early on, a problem about a ramp and a problem about a spring seem like two different problems. Over time the student sees the principle they share, and the ramp and the spring fade into the background.2 It's the same kind of filtering Freytag's brain returned to as it healed: keeping the relationships and letting the surface detail go. The result is a larger structure that wasn't there before, built partly from what the student already knew and partly from what they've just learned.

DiSessa and Bruce Sherin later gave this connected knowledge a name. In a 1998 paper, they called it a coordination class: the ability to recognize a concept like force across situations that look nothing alike, and to reason about each one consistently.8 The guitarist who can find a song anywhere on the neck has made the same kind of connection, and what they know in one place now carries over to the next.

Since the earliest computer tutors of the 1970s, a common approach to building learning software has been to map out what an expert knows and then measure the learner against that map, checking off each piece as it's mastered. Researchers call this an overlay model, because the learner's knowledge is treated as a partial copy laid over the expert's.9 It's a sensible design, and it has worked well in some subjects. If diSessa is right, though, a learner isn't a smaller version of an expert. Their knowledge is organized differently, in pieces that work in some places and not others, and the real work of learning happens in how those pieces get connected, sharpened and set aside. A tool that only checks off what's been covered may never see that work happening.

Connecting the pieces

There's an irony here. Decades after researchers came to see human knowledge as pieces that need connecting, the AI tools now being built into learning fit that description almost perfectly. A large language model has absorbed an enormous amount of human writing, and it can produce fluent explanations of nearly anything. What it holds, though, is closer to a vast collection of pieces than a single organized understanding. Ask it the same question two different ways and you can get answers that don't quite agree, each drawing on a different part of what it has seen. The pieces get pulled together fresh each time, shaped by the question in front of it.

Pulling pieces together is a kind of interpretation, and there's rarely just one right way to do it. Think of an orchestra. Every musician in it is skilled, and the score is the same from night to night, but the performance depends on the conductor. Two conductors can take the same symphony and produce two different performances, both faithful and both good. What separates them are the choices each one makes in the moment, like letting the strings swell or quieting them so a melody in the woodwinds can come through. Integrating knowledge works this way too. Faced with a new problem, a student has to decide which of their intuitions to bring forward and which to quiet, the way a physics student learns when the push intuition helps and when it gets in the way. An expert makes those choices so fluidly they hardly notice. A language model's answers involve something similar: each one brings some pieces forward and leaves others out, shaped by the question it was asked. For a learner using one, that raises a practical question. If the tool is doing that sorting, when does the learner practice it?

What conversations reveal

The conversations people have with AI do more than produce answers. They show how the person asking is thinking. A student who explains a problem in their own words, takes a guess, or pushes back on a response is showing the pieces they're working with. And these conversations are becoming ordinary. A 2025 Common Sense Media survey found that talking with AI companions is already a regular habit for many teenagers10, and as voice tools improve, it feels less like searching and more like talking something through.

An overlay tutor starts with the expert's map and asks what the learner is missing. A different kind of tool could start with the learner's pieces and ask how they fit together. Say a student tells an AI tutor that a heavier ball falls faster, and a few minutes later correctly explains why two balls of different weights land at the same time. An overlay tutor would mark one answer wrong and the other right. A tool built around pieces might ask the student when each answer would be right. Heavier things really do fall faster when air resistance matters, as with a rock and a feather dropped together, so the intuition stays, limited to the situations where it belongs.

Recognizing a learner's pieces is a hard problem. Students rarely state their intuitions outright. DiSessa and his colleagues drew them out through hours of interviews, asking follow-up questions and watching how a student's reasoning shifted from one situation to the next. Even then, the evidence is indirect. The same sentence can come from very different ways of thinking, and the same intuition can show up in words that sound nothing alike. There is also no complete list of intuitions to check against. Researchers have mapped some of them carefully in physics, and far fewer in most other subjects. A tool that claims to see a learner's pieces is making an inference. Few tools have been tested to see whether their readings match what a careful interviewer would find, or whether they would reach the same reading twice.

Some of that risk falls unevenly. A model that learned mostly from polished writing may treat textbook phrasing as a sign of understanding and everyday phrasing as a sign of confusion. Students who already sound like school would get the benefit of the doubt. Students who explain things in their own words would get misread more often, and they are often the same learners traditional assessment has underestimated.

Those problems point to a more basic one: how a tool would capture a learner's intuitions and pieces of knowledge, and follow how the connections between them change over time. A tool built around pieces would need a picture of how one learner's knowledge is organized: the intuitions they bring, the situations where each one shows up, and the connections they've started to make. Researchers have taken early steps toward this. More than a decade ago, Bruce Sherin used simple computer analysis on interviews in which 54 middle school students explained the seasons, and the computer picked out student ideas that lined up with much of what human researchers had found.11 Today's language models can do much more with a learner's words, during the conversation itself.

A learner's conversations with AI leave something like an imprint of how their knowledge is organized. That imprint can show which intuitions a learner reaches for, and in which situations. It can also show how they assemble an explanation, settling on one idea and then shifting to another, much as Sherin and his colleagues saw students do in their interviews.12 It shows the pieces more clearly than the connections between them, though. Those connections become visible only as a learner reasons consistently across many different situations, which a single conversation rarely captures. And the imprint is made by both sides of the conversation. The AI's questions decide which pieces get called up, and those questions aren't neutral. They're shaped by what the model learned from its training data and by the choices its designers made about how it should respond. What the imprint shows about the learner is partly shaped by those influences.

The layer underneath

Freytag's injury gave a rare look at something usually hidden. Beneath the ordinary way of hearing, organized around how notes relate to each other, sat a capacity his brain had been filtering out, one that may be present in many of us.1, 13 DiSessa's interviews expose a similar layer in how people understand the world: small intuitions, each useful somewhere, that become invisible once they're woven into a larger understanding. The woven-together result is what we usually call knowledge. The pieces underneath could have been connected in other ways, and in different people they are.

Most learning tools have worked only with the finished structure, measuring learners against one expert's map. AI opens up the layer underneath. It holds an enormous range of pieces, and through conversation it catches some of a learner's own, however partially. That raises a question learning design has rarely had reason to ask. If knowledge can be connected in many ways, how might a tool help each learner build a structure that holds together, starting from the pieces they bring, without settling in advance what that structure should look like?

Answering it would mean treating the layer Freytag's injury and diSessa's interviews briefly made visible as the place where learning begins. Experts in a field share its deep patterns, yet they connect them in different ways, the way two conductors can both do justice to the same score. AI gives us a way to see more of how each learner's knowledge is coming together, and to support it as it takes its own shape. That is an invitation to rethink what education could look like when it starts from the pieces each learner brings.
