How This Essay Was Written

A companion to Learning in Pieces: What a Brain Injury Reveals About Knowledge and AI.

I wrote this essay with Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, over a long conversation across two days, some of it typed and much of it spoken aloud.

It began loosely. I summarized the Times article about Edward Freytag for Claude and mentioned Oliver Sacks, and Claude offered connections to the learning sciences. Its first full drafts missed. One assumed readers already knew the field, and the next was readable but didn't sound like me. So we changed how we worked. Claude proposed one passage at a time, and I said what wasn't working: a sentence that only existed to set up the next one, a term a general reader wouldn't know, a leap the argument hadn't earned. We went around again, sometimes a dozen times on a single paragraph.

Ideas moved in both directions. I brought the frame: language models as disconnected knowledge, an argument that builds step by step, and a question left open at the end. Claude brought research I hadn't planned to use, like Bruce Sherin's computer analysis of student interviews and the history of early computer tutors. Some of what it offered came out in revision, including brain scans of jazz improvisers and a jigsaw-puzzle metaphor that needed too much explaining. When I asked Claude to explain its reasoning, it sometimes turned out to be reaching. Together we worked out how diSessa's "knowledge in pieces" could hold the essay together.

Near the end, the roles shifted. I rewrote passages myself, and Claude checked them: fixing grammar, telling me when a paragraph didn't sound like me, testing the essay's claims about Freytag against the Times article, and reading the full draft for repetition and contradictions.

Looking back, the process resembled the essay's own argument. Claude offered a wide range of pieces. Deciding how they fit together, and what the essay would say, stayed with me.
